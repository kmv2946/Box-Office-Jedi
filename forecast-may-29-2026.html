FORECAST: In the ‘Backrooms’ of the Box Office

by Box Office Jedi

Thursday, May 28, 2026

The box office hasn’t been this exciting in a long time, and it’s all happened within a few weeks. Michael and Obsession are two of the most interesting box office stories of the 2020s, and Backrooms is poised to continue the streak.

Each of those three films are worthy stories on their own, but there’s a broader phenomenon at play here. Those three unexpected successes are a collective foil to the movies the industry thought would be the headline. Last weekend, The Mandalorian and Grogu posted the lowest opening for any Star Wars film and was overshadowed in the conversation by Obsession’s runaway hold. The Super Mario Galaxy Movie and The Devil Wears Prada 2 are each doing well in absolute terms, but neither feels especially exciting. Mario is tracking about $100 million below its predecessor. Prada 2, despite being a sizable increase over the 2006 original, fell short of the more bullish projections of a $100 million-plus opening that would have made for a real story. Michael, which debuted smack-dab between those two tentpoles, emerged as the top story.

Anyone actually paying attention to the culture could’ve seen most of this coming. Michael’s success has a sort-of silent-majority energy carrying it through its sixth weekend, reminiscent of The Passion of the Christ. Both films, as we noted in our roundup, had finished at exactly $315 million by the end of their fifth weekends.

But Backrooms and Obsession feel, to the untrained eye, more like sorcery.

Their box office comes from a place that seems off-limits to the average industry observer, not unlike the Backrooms itself: that internet-born mythology of endless fluorescent corridors, the liminal layer of reality where things slip out of public sight. To most, it’s just unused office space. To others, it’s full of meaning and potential and lore. There’s a parallel ecosystem of audience attention that doesn’t show up in trailer-view dashboards or paid-search tracking. It lives in Discord servers, TikTok comments, late-night YouTube binges, Reddit theory threads, and group chats. Things grow there, away from anyone who’s counting, and then they emerge fully-formed on the box office charts.

The internet has shaped box office before. The Blair Witch Project, in 1999, ran on a website that pretended the events were real, the digital equivalent of a campfire rumor that no one could verify. The internet then was lawless and unbranded, and there were no studios or institutions tampering with the narratives, so the spread of the Blair Witch mythology felt authentic in a way studio marketing never could. Napoleon Dynamite, five years later, became a phenomenon through quotable AIM away messages, college-dorm word of mouth, and a generation of Vote for Pedro t-shirts. Snakes on a Plane, in 2006, was the famous cautionary tale: the internet wanted that movie made, fan-created marketing surged ahead of release, the studio added scenes based on fan suggestions, and then it opened to $15 million.

What Backrooms, Iron Lung, Obsession, and (to some extent) Five Nights at Freddy’s represent is something different. These films aren’t using the internet to amplify a marketing campaign. They are born from the internet, from creepypasta, from YouTube horror shorts, from video game franchises that were digital-native from day one. If you’ve ever been to a cast-and-crew screening, or a film festival premiere, you know the type of hysterical enthusiasm we’re talking about. Those screenings are wild because everyone in the audience has some kind of stake in the film. With a film like Backrooms, everyone who’s ever participated in a Reddit thread might as well feel like they played a part in its production. It’s the biggest friend-and-family screening ever.

The comps for Backrooms are all over the place. Iron Lung and Obsession opened to $18.2M and $17.2M respectively. Five Nights at Freddy’s opened to $80M. Going further back, The Blair Witch Project’s first wide weekend came in at $29.2M, or a whopping $64.4M adjusted for 2026 ticket prices.

It’s also worth stacking up Backrooms with some of the major horror openings. It has a good shot at breaking into the top 10 horror openings of all time. The current list looks like this:

  1. It (2017) — $123,403,419
  2. It Chapter Two (2019) — $91,062,152
  3. The Conjuring: Last Rites (2025) — $84,006,121
  4. Five Nights at Freddy’s (2023) — $80,001,720
  5. Halloween (2018) — $76,221,545
  6. Us (2019) — $71,117,625
  7. Five Nights at Freddy’s 2 (2025) — $64,007,430
  8. Scream 7 (2026) — $63,615,172
  9. The Nun (2018) — $53,807,379
  10. Paranormal Activity 3 (2011) — $52,568,183

This is debatable, but I’ve left out Signs ($60.1M, more sci-fi thriller) and Hannibal ($58.0M, more psychological thriller).

At the end of the day, how well will Backrooms and Obsession play together? There is no failure story here. The question is just how big a success we’re talking about.

Has Obsession primed audiences to crave more internet-minded horror fare, teeing up Backrooms for a bigger-than-expected bow? Or in a surprise twist, has Obsession hoarded the audience that Backrooms was counting on to overperform? Will Obsession continue to defy expectations in its third weekend (just look at its weekday numbers compared to last week’s…), or will Backrooms steal the spotlight just enough to render Obsession a fad?

We’re feeling like we’re about to have our expectations shattered all over again, with both films soaring this weekend, propelled by a collective sense that, if only for a few days, the movieplex belongs to the internet.
