Forecast: Fall Preview

by Box Office Jedi

September 14, 2026

After a relatively monumental summer, the movies are taking a classic autumn breather. That used to be normal until the late 2010s, when studios realized they could open tentpole releases in the off months (It Chapter One, It Chapter Two, Joker, Venom, Beetlejuice Beetlejuice). Last September saw two movies open above $70 million in the first two weeks (The Conjuring: Last Rites, Demon Slayer). This year, the only movie that seems poised to come close to that is Resident Evil.

More than anything, these coming months are filler between two matching bookends—each pairing a prestige IMAX event with a Marvel heavy-hitter. Behind us we have The Odyssey and Spider-Man: Brand New Day. Ahead of us, in December, we have Dune: Part Three and Avengers: Doomsday.

And these movies fall in the wake of a lot of industry upheaval. Obsession and Backrooms have sparked renewed interest in new(ish) talent. Paramount cleared a major hurdle in its bid to buy Warner Bros. Donald Trump is pushing a federal tax credit for Hollywood. And on humanity’s behalf, Ben Stiller and Mark Ruffalo have linked arms with Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon in the good fight against Artificial Intelligence.

And so the movies have gone quiet, for now. The festival circuit, too. Unlike the year Anora, The Brutalist, and Emilia Pérez emerged to reshape the Oscar narrative, nothing this year has shaken up the race. It was all decided in July.

While we don’t have it ranked in our projections, the event of the season is surely A24’s You Can See Everything. After a loud, surprise premiere at Telluride, the teaser trailer went viral, sparking a lot of speculation around the film. We didn’t rank it because it’s hard to say how far it will reach without knowing A24’s rollout plan. There has only been one documentary to really transcend a limited audience: Fahrenheit 9/11 grossed $119.2 million domestic in 2004. Two, if you count March of the Penguins’ $77.4 million haul a year later. You Can See Everything also reminds us of Borat—a niche comic who found his way to a mass audience. Borat and Fahrenheit 9/11 rolled out almost identically, opening moderately wide, around 800 theaters, then expanding into the thousands. Borat stacked back-to-back $25-million-plus weekends ($26.4M, then $29M as it widened); Fahrenheit 9/11 opened just under that, at $23.9 million, and became the highest-grossing traditional documentary ever. That’s the sweet spot for You Can See Everything, rather than keeping it in platform release its first couple of weeks. While it could very well end up closer to Bowling for Columbine ($21.6 million domestic total), we absolutely live in a world where a three-hour documentary opens above $25 million and lands a Best Picture nomination.

Here are our predictions for the top ten grossers in the coming months, including our guess for opening weekend and domestic total gross.

1. The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping November 20

The original Hunger Games, from March 2012, was the peak moment of the YA adaptation era. The Harry Potter franchise had concluded in 2011, the Twilight saga was ending in November 2012, and The Hunger Games launched on the crest of their wave. But since then, YA adaptations have dwindled. The final Hunger Games movie fizzled, and the Divergent franchise didn’t even give its final film a theatrical release. So no one was expecting fireworks from 2023’s The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, but it had decent legs, winding up with a domestic total of $161 million (roughly Catching Fire’s opening weekend gross).

That number feels like the floor here. Since 2023, there’s been a palpable nostalgia for the 2012–2016 era, when the original Hunger Games movies came out. It’s not a quantifiable nostalgia, but it does seem to be lending this installment something magnetic.

And with no real competition until December 18th, expect it to leg out.

Opening Weekend: $65 million
Domestic Total: $180 million
2. Resident Evil September 18

Probably the easiest layup of the season. While this prediction is technically an overperformance, there’d be nothing surprising about it.

Opening Weekend: $60 million
Domestic Total: $144 million
3. Verity October 2

We consider this a conservative prediction. There’s a perfect storm here: the Anne Hathaway–Dakota Johnson pairing echoes the Amanda Seyfried–Sydney Sweeney two-hander from The Housemaid, last holiday season’s runaway hit—but with arguably more compelling leads.

Opening Weekend: $46 million
Domestic Total: $137 million
4. Other Mommy October 9

Horror is kind of becoming one of the only reasons for people to go to the movies anymore. All you need to do to overperform is not completely fumble the marketing. Other Mommy seems like it’s falling somewhere between Smile and Weapons, which is a great place to be. This also benefits from a lack of competition—there are literally no wide releases currently slated for Halloween weekend. It could only suffer from showing too much in its later trailers.

Opening Weekend: $33 million
Domestic Total: $90 million
5. Hexed November 25

What is this movie? Random and totally safe prediction here. Original animation is generally in trouble, with DreamWorks’ Forgotten Island not even making this list.

Opening Weekend: $26 million
Domestic Total: $86 million
6. Focker-In-Law November 25

This is a pretty generous prediction. We don’t want to write it off completely, since there’s still goodwill around the original movie, but Robert De Niro and Ben Stiller have both driven themselves into irrelevance more so than nearly any other A-lister. If this IP struggled in 2010—Little Fockers ended its run at just over half of its predecessor ($148 million to Meet the Fockers’ $279 million)—it will certainly have trouble in 2026. The floor is very low with this one.

Opening Weekend: $29 million
Domestic Total: $82 million
7. Godzilla Minus Zero November 6

This is a real wildcard. The trailer touting “the first Japanese production filmed for IMAX,” with the promise of Ghidorah, suggests a bump from Godzilla Minus One. But unlike the Godzilla x Kong movies Warner Bros has been putting out, Godzilla Minus Zero feels like it has the potential to transcend the kaiju fanbase and net a more general base of nerds. People are rooting for this iteration of Godzilla. Godzilla Minus One’s Best Visual Effects win kind of says it all.

It could go any way, but our guess feels like it’s on the conservative side. If there’s real traction in the nerd community, this could reach a $40–50 million opening weekend.

Opening Weekend: $27 million
Domestic Total: $70 million
8. Heart of the Beast September 25

One for the flyover states.

Opening Weekend: $19 million
Domestic Total: $70 million
9. Primetime September 25

Doing what The Smashing Machine was supposed to do last year.

Opening Weekend: $18 million
Domestic Total: $60 million
10. Digger October 2

This will be a close-to-exact repeat of One Battle After Another, except it’s not winning Best Picture (it probably won’t even get nominated). With an unrecognizable Tom Cruise, two-time Best Director winner Iñárritu sets out to repeat what he did with Leonardo DiCaprio in The Revenant, but a decade later, people can see through Iñárritu’s pseudo-sophistication and, frankly, they don’t care. This is a generous prediction.

Opening Weekend: $21 million
Domestic Total: $59 million
* * *

There are also a handful of titles this season that could very well end their domestic runs below $20 million, but have the potential to break out. Wildwood is LAIKA’s latest, and while the studio hasn’t had Pixar-level success at the domestic box office, its more artisanal approach has earned it a good reputation. The 15th-anniversary run of Coraline played spectacularly for a re-release ($33 million domestic) and points to a real devotion to the studio that could pay off for Wildwood. Whalefall features Austin Abrams leading another very straightforward race for survival—this time clawing his way out of the belly of a whale, in a tight runtime. Whales aren’t as proven as sharks, but this should draw the same crowd. Expect an opening around $6–8 million, but if the stars align, a $16–18 million breakout in line with The Shallows (2016) isn’t out of the question. I Play Rocky feels like it’s striking a nerve, and could feasibly make a lot of money and land a Best Picture nomination. Realistically, it’ll play moderately well for a festival movie ($40–50 million domestic) and settle for a Golden Globe nomination. Ebenezer, the Johnny Depp reboot, leans heavily on the return of “the master of misfits,” citing the rowdy characters he’s given us through the ages. We don’t know why Disney didn’t just let him go straight to reprising Jack Sparrow. Just kidding—this is Paramount.

Likely bombs include Forgotten Island, The Cat in the Hat, Clayface, Street Fighter, and, finally, The Social Reckoning. What could have been a major event—and should have been called The Social Network 2—instead gets a knee-jerk Hollywood approach from Aaron Sorkin (like this season’s other billionaire tell-alls, Ink, Artificial, and Musk) that cheapens the whole thing. This will benefit Nathan Fielder.

Let’s assume You Can See Everything opens to 800 theaters, then expands to 2,100 in its second week. We take a chance and guess a $16 million opening weekend ($20,000 per screen), and a $14 million second weekend. That could be enough for a number one debut.
